The mechanics

Two algorithms. Very different problems.

Most coverage of quantum risk is vague. The actual picture is specific, and the difference between these two determines what you have to do.

Shor's algorithm — existential

Solves factorisation and discrete logarithms in polynomial time. That is the exact mathematics every public-key algorithm in mainstream use depends on: RSA, DSA, Diffie-Hellman, ECDSA, ECDH, Ed25519. Against a quantum computer their security is not reduced. It is eliminated.

Grover's algorithm — manageable

Gives a quadratic speed-up on search, which halves the effective strength of a symmetric cipher. AES-128 drops to roughly 64 bits and must go. AES-256 drops to 128 and remains sound. This part of the problem is a configuration change, not a programme.

The consequence — retrospective

Because traffic can be recorded now and decrypted later, your exposure is not set by when quantum computers arrive. It is set by how long your data must stay confidential. For patient records, legal files or pension data, that window opened years ago.

The point most people miss

A bigger key does not help. RSA-15360 falls to the same algorithm as RSA-2048 — it simply takes marginally longer. Migration means replacing algorithms, not resizing them. Any vendor telling you to "increase key length" for quantum readiness has misunderstood the threat.

What this does not tell you

The inequality gives you urgency, not an inventory. It cannot say which of your certificates use RSA, which endpoints lack forward secrecy, or which suppliers will still be on classical cryptography in 2031. That is what the scan is for.

The assessment

What we actually look at

Evidence, not a questionnaire. We connect to your estate and read what your servers really negotiate.

Certificates

Full chain retrieval, key algorithm and strength, signature algorithm, expiry and lifetime, trust and hostname validation, chain completeness, Certificate Transparency, wildcard blast radius.

TLS configuration

Real protocol negotiation from SSLv3 to TLS 1.3, complete cipher suite enumeration, forward secrecy, and the broken, export, anonymous and NULL suites that should have gone a decade ago.

Post-quantum readiness

We probe directly for hybrid ML-KEM key exchange — X25519MLKEM768 and its siblings — plus superseded Kyber drafts. This is the measurement that tells you whether today's traffic survives.

Licences and components

Third-party components and versions mapped to SPDX licences, flagging what actually bites: the AGPL network clause, SSPL, GPL obligations, commercial-use restrictions and end-of-life libraries.

DNS and mail posture

CAA, DNSSEC, SPF and DMARC policy strength. Domain spoofing is behind most invoice fraud, and it damages a brand faster than almost any technical flaw.

Compliance mapping

Findings mapped to the regimes you are judged by: NCSC's roadmap, NIST FIPS 203/204/205, CNSA 2.0, PCI DSS 4.0, UK GDPR Article 32, NIS2, ISO 27001:2022 and Cyber Essentials.

What an external scan cannot see

Internal systems, VPNs, SSH, code signing, database and backup encryption, and hardware security modules are invisible from outside — and they are usually where the difficult cases live. The free scan covers your public estate. The paid assessment is where we go inside, and it is what the NCSC 2028 discovery phase actually requires.

NCSC migration roadmap

Three dates that are already fixed

The National Cyber Security Centre has set a phased national timeline. Working backwards from 2035 leaves less room than it appears.

2028

Discover and plan

Complete a cryptographic inventory of every system and service that depends on cryptography, and draft a migration plan. You cannot replace what you cannot enumerate — which is why this comes first.

2031

Migrate the priorities

Complete the highest-priority migration activities, starting with anything protecting long-lived confidential data. This is where the harvest-now-decrypt-later exposure is actually closed.

2035

Finish

All systems, services and products migrated to post-quantum cryptography. Under NIST's parallel timeline, today's 112-bit algorithms are disallowed entirely from this point.
